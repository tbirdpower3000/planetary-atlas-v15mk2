Mars through time and Mars minerals

The timeline, Ancient Mars, the northern ocean, the major impacts and the OMEGA mineral maps, from the Mars Planetary Modeling Atlas.

  1. Genova, A. et al. (2016). Seasonal and static gravity field of Mars from MGS, Mars Odyssey and MRO radio science. Icarus 272, 228–245. doiThe GMM-3 gravity model. Summed to spherical-harmonic degree 89 to make the areoid on the globe, the rover-site areoid heights, and every Ancient Mars view.
  2. Smith, D.E. et al. (2001). Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter: experiment summary after the first year of global mapping of Mars. J. Geophys. Res. 106, 23689–23722.MOLA topography, as the MarsTopo719 spherical-harmonic model distributed with SHTools. It is the Topography surface map and the Elevation column: the model's radius minus the GMM-3 areoid, sampled every 0.25°. At its 15 km resolution it smooths narrow features such as Gale's floor, so for the four rovers with a walkable terrain patch the Elevation column uses the 128-pixel-per-degree MOLA MEGDR instead, which gives −2,571 m for Perseverance and −4,503 m for Curiosity against NASA's −2,570 m and −4,501 m. Also used to measure the Hellas ellipse's orientation and to test a topography-only Tharsis model, rejected because it explained only about 40% of Tharsis's gravity.
  3. Smith, D.E., Neumann, G.A. et al. (2003). Mars Global Surveyor Laser Altimeter Mission Experiment Gridded Data Record, MGS-M-MOLA-5-MEGDR-L3-V1.0. NASA Planetary Data System (Geosciences Node). pds-geosciences.wustl.eduThe 128 pixel-per-degree (about 460 m) topography under every Walk on Mars site: a patch 160–320 km across around each site, read directly from the megt*hb.img tiles. It gives −4,503 m at Curiosity's landing point and −2,571 m at Perseverance's, against NASA's recorded −4,501 m and −2,570 m.
  4. Konopliv, A.S. et al. (2016). An improved JPL Mars gravity field and orientation from Mars orbiter and lander tracking data. Icarus 274, 253–260.Mars's moment-of-inertia factor, 0.3644, which sets the rotational bulge (J2 = 1.820 × 10⁻³) that the Ancient Mars views are measured against.
  5. Stähler, S.C. et al. (2021). Seismic detection of the martian core. Science 373, 443–448.The InSight core radius, about 1,830 km, drawn to scale inside the globe.
  6. Wieczorek, M.A. & Meschede, M. (2018). SHTools: Tools for working with spherical harmonics. Geochem. Geophys. Geosyst. 19, 2574–2592.The software used to expand the gravity and topography models, compute the areoid and remove the Tharsis, Hellas and Argyre mass.
  7. Wordsworth, R. et al. (2021). A coupled model of episodic warming, oxidation and geochemical transitions on early Mars. Nature Geoscience 14, 127–132. doiFig. 1a is the geologic timeline: every bar, era boundary and the dates for Tharsis, Hellas (4.16 Ga) and Argyre (3.98 Ga) on the globe and in Play the timeline. Bar positions were read off the figure to about ±0.03 Ga.
  8. Andrews-Hanna, J.C., Zuber, M.T. & Banerdt, W.B. (2008). The Borealis basin and the origin of the martian crustal dichotomy. Nature 453, 1212–1215. doiThe Borealis ellipse (10,600 × 8,500 km, 67° N 208° E, N76°E) and its two alternative fits; the Arabia Terra outer ring at 1.57 radii; Hellas's axes (2,414 × 1,820 km); and the case for removing Tharsis as a flexurally supported load in Ancient Mars.
  9. Melosh, H.J. (2008). Did an impact blast away half of the martian crust? Nature Geoscience 1, 412–414.The 4.4 Ga Borealis date, the impactor sizes and speeds in the Borealis impact energy calculator, and the binding-energy comparison it checks (1.6% for a 1,000 km rock at 10 km/s, against the paper's 1.5%).
  10. Bouley, S. et al. (2016). Late Tharsis formation and implications for early Mars. Nature 531, 344–347. doiThe published 20–25° true polar wander, shown as the second ancient spin-axis option. Numbers taken from the paper's press summary, as the paper itself is paywalled.
  11. Robbins, S.J., Hynek, B.M., Lillis, R.J. & Bottke, W.F. (2013). Large impact crater histories of Mars: the effect of different model crater age techniques. Icarus 225, 173–184. doiIsochron ages for Ladon, Prometheus, Hellas, Isidis and Argyre (Table 2 and §5.1) and Gale (§4.4); relative ages for Huygens, Antoniadi, Copernicus and Holden; Lyot's era. Each range spans the paper's Hartmann and Neukum ages.
  12. Bottke, W.F. & Andrews-Hanna, J.C. (2017). A post-accretionary lull in large impacts on early Mars. Nature Geoscience 10, 344–348, and Supplementary Information.Ladon basin's centre and size, and the 4.1–4.4 Ga window for Utopia.
  13. Frey, H.V. (2004). Impact constraints on the age and origin of the crustal dichotomy on Mars. Workshop on Hemispheres Apart, LPI Contribution 1213.The 4.04–4.20 Ga model age for the lowland basins, used for Utopia.
  14. IAU / USGS Gazetteer of Planetary Nomenclature. planetarynames.wr.usgs.govCentres and diameters of the named craters and basins, and the Tharsis Montes centre, read through the Wikipedia crater lists that reproduce it.
  15. Citron, R.I., Manga, M. & Hemingway, D.J. (2018). Timing of oceans on Mars from shoreline deformation. Nature 555, 643–646. doiThe Tharsis correction for the ocean and the shoreline ages.
  16. Carr, M.H. & Head, J.W. (2003). Oceans on Mars: an assessment of the observational evidence and possible fate. J. Geophys. Res. 108, 5042. doiThe Arabia and Deuteronilus contacts.
  17. Sholes, S.F., Dickeson, Z.I., Montgomery, D.R. & Catling, D.C. (2021). Where are Mars’ hypothesized ocean shorelines? Large lateral and topographic offsets between different versions of paleoshoreline maps. J. Geophys. Res. Planets 126, e2020JE006486. doiThe compiled shoreline points and their mean heights.
  18. Scheller, E.L., Ehlmann, B.L., Hu, R., Adams, D.J. & Yung, Y.L. (2021). Long-term drying of Mars by sequestration of ocean-scale volumes of water in the crust. Science 372, 56–62. doiWhen the ocean dries out on the timeline.
  19. Ody, A., Poulet, F., Langevin, Y. et al. (2012). Global maps of anhydrous minerals at the surface of Mars from OMEGA/MEx. J. Geophys. Res. 117, E00J14. doiThe Minerals layer: ferric oxides (BD530), dust (NNPHS), pyroxene (BD2000), olivine (OSP1) and near-infrared albedo (R1080).
